**7. REGULATION AND THE CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK**

Theory: 
Accounting regulation developed because businesses became more complex and accountants needed common rules for recording and reporting transactions. Before formal accounting standards, accounting relied on generally accepted accounting principles, or GAAP. GAAP refers to the rules, practices and procedures that became accepted through repeated use by accountants and the business community. The theoretical issue is that without stronger regulation, too much choice exists in accounting, and that choice can reduce comparability, reduce reliability, and allow financial reports to be shaped in ways that favour management rather than users.

The lecture explains that regulation is needed because financial reports are used by people who are not inside the business. Shareholders, creditors, investors, employees, government and other users rely on financial statements to make economic decisions. Because those users cannot usually access all internal information, accounting standards force entities to prepare reports in a structured and consistent way. Regulation therefore protects users by reducing misleading reporting, improving disclosure, and making financial information more comparable across entities and across time.

The main Australian regulatory structure includes the Financial Reporting Council, the Australian Accounting Standards Board, ASIC, and the ASX. The Financial Reporting Council oversees and advises the AASB and the Auditing and Assurance Standards Board. The AASB issues Australian accounting standards and is central to the standard-setting process. ASIC enforces company law, protects investors and consumers, administers legal requirements, processes company information, and supports market integrity. The ASX influences accounting because listed companies must disclose information properly to the market. Together, these bodies create, supervise, and enforce the reporting environment.

The international side of regulation is also important. The IASB develops international accounting standards, and Australia generally adopts international standards into the Australian reporting system. The IFRS Interpretations Committee deals with issues where accounting standards are unclear, incomplete, or being interpreted inconsistently. Its role is to provide authoritative guidance so that similar transactions are treated consistently. The FASB is the United States standard-setting body, and although the US does not fully follow IFRS, convergence between US standards and international standards has been an important long-term aim.

The Conceptual Framework is the theory base behind financial reporting. It is not simply a list of rules. It explains the objective of financial reporting, the users of financial reports, the qualities that useful information should have, the definitions of assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses, and the principles for recognising and measuring those elements. Its purpose is to help standard setters create consistent standards, help accountants deal with areas where no specific standard exists, and help users understand the logic behind financial reporting.

The Conceptual Framework is important because accounting standards cannot cover every possible transaction in advance. Business transactions change over time, and new industries, new financial instruments, and new business practices constantly appear. The framework provides a consistent reasoning structure. Instead of asking only “what rule applies?”, accountants also ask whether the item meets an element definition, whether recognising it gives relevant and faithfully represented information, and what measurement basis gives useful information.

The lecture describes the development of the Conceptual Framework as a staged process. First, it defines the boundary of financial reporting by focusing on general purpose financial reporting. Second, it defines the reporting entity. Third, it establishes the objectives of general purpose financial reporting by identifying users and their information needs. Fourth, it develops the qualitative characteristics, elements, recognition principles and measurement concepts that guide the preparation of financial reports.

General purpose financial reporting means preparing one set of financial reports designed to meet the common information needs of many users. The theory is that it would be inefficient and impractical to prepare a different financial report for every separate user group. General purpose reports therefore provide broad financial information useful to existing and potential investors, lenders, creditors, employees, government and other users. However, they do not provide all information users need, so users must also consider other economic, political, industry and entity-specific information.

A reporting entity is an entity that has users who depend on general purpose financial reports for information. SAC 1 identifies indicators that help determine whether an entity is a reporting entity. These include separation of management from economic interest, economic or political importance, and financial characteristics. The theoretical logic is that when owners, investors or creditors are separate from management, they cannot directly observe management’s actions, so they depend on formal financial reports.

The lecture links reporting entities to agency theory. Agency theory recognises that managers and owners may not always have identical interests. Managers control the business and often control the preparation of information, while shareholders and creditors rely on reported information to assess performance and financial position. Because of this separation, financial reporting rules are needed to reduce information asymmetry and to limit opportunistic reporting.

The objective of financial reporting is to provide useful financial information for decision-making. Users need information about both financial position and financial performance. Financial position refers to the entity’s assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. Financial performance refers to income, expenses, and profit or loss over a period. Together, these help users assess liquidity, solvency, profitability, efficiency, risk, future cash-generating ability and management stewardship.

The Conceptual Framework identifies qualitative characteristics that make financial information useful. The two fundamental qualitative characteristics are relevance and faithful representation. Information is relevant when it can influence users’ decisions. Faithful representation means information is complete, neutral and free from material error. These two characteristics are fundamental because information that is irrelevant is not useful, and information that is not faithfully represented can mislead users.

The enhancing qualitative characteristics are comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability. Comparability allows users to identify similarities and differences between entities and between reporting periods. Verifiability means independent and knowledgeable observers could reach reasonable agreement that the information faithfully represents what it claims to represent. Timeliness means information is available early enough to influence decisions. Understandability means information is presented clearly, while still recognising that some accounting matters are inherently complex.

The lecture also emphasises the cost constraint. Financial reporting creates costs, including collecting, processing, storing, auditing, presenting and interpreting information. The Conceptual Framework requires that the benefits of providing information should justify the costs. This means not every possible piece of information should be reported. Information should be reported when it is useful enough to justify the cost of producing and disclosing it.

The framework defines the five elements of financial statements: assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses. An asset is a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. A liability is a present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events. Equity is the residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting liabilities. Income is increases in assets or decreases in liabilities that increase equity, excluding owner contributions. Expenses are decreases in assets or increases in liabilities that decrease equity, excluding distributions to owners.

The definitions matter because accounting does not record something simply because cash moved or because management wants to show it. The item must fit the definition of an element. The most important starting point is assets and liabilities, because equity is defined as assets minus liabilities, and income and expenses are defined through changes in assets and liabilities. This means modern financial reporting is strongly built around identifying present resources and present obligations.

Recognition means including an item in the financial statements with a monetary amount. It is not enough for something to be discussed or known; recognition means it is formally recorded in the statement of financial position or statement of financial performance. Under the Conceptual Framework, an asset or liability is recognised when recognition provides relevant information, faithfully represents the item and related changes, and satisfies the cost-benefit constraint.

Recognition does not require absolute certainty. Accounting often uses estimates. However, if uncertainty is too high, recognition may not provide useful information. The lecture explains that some items may meet part of the definition of an element but still not be recognised because existence, outcome, probability, or measurement is too uncertain. When an item is too uncertain to recognise, it may still need disclosure in the notes if the information is relevant to users.

Revenue recognition is connected to the Conceptual Framework because income is recognised only when the required conditions are satisfied. The lecture refers to the revenue recognition logic that requires an arrangement with enforceable rights, identifiable obligations, identifiable payment terms, commercial substance, and probable collection. The key theory is that revenue is not recognised merely because the business hopes to receive cash; it is recognised when the entity has a valid basis for reporting an increase in assets or decrease in liabilities that increases equity.

Expense recognition is also based on the definitions of elements. Expenses are recognised when assets decrease or liabilities increase in a way that decreases equity, excluding owner distributions. The lecture stresses that expenses do not have to involve immediate cash payment. Expenses can arise from the consumption of economic benefits, the incurrence of obligations, or accounting allocations that represent the use of assets over time. This separates accrual accounting from simple cash accounting.

Measurement is the process of assigning monetary amounts to recognised elements. It is important because the value chosen for assets and liabilities affects equity, profit, financial position and user perceptions. The lecture identifies major measurement bases including historical cost, fair value, value in use and current cost. The theoretical issue is that the same item can produce different reported values depending on the measurement basis selected. Therefore, accounting measurement is not just arithmetic; it involves selecting an appropriate basis that provides useful information.

Historical cost measures an asset based on the original transaction amount, adjusted where relevant for depreciation, amortisation, impairment or other reductions. Its theoretical strength is verifiability because it is usually supported by transaction evidence. Its weakness is that it may become less relevant over time because old costs may no longer represent current economic value.

Fair value measures an asset or liability based on the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction. Its theoretical strength is relevance because it reflects current economic conditions. Its weakness is that where active markets do not exist, fair value may require estimates and judgement, which can reduce verifiability.

Value in use measures an asset by reference to the present value of future economic benefits expected from using it. It is based on expected future cash flows or economic benefits discounted to present value. Its theoretical focus is internal usefulness to the entity rather than immediate sale value. It requires judgement about future benefits, timing, risk and discount rates.

Current cost measures an asset according to the amount that would be required to replace it with an equivalent asset. Its theoretical focus is the cost of maintaining productive capacity. It is useful when the concern is not what the asset originally cost or what it could be sold for, but what it would cost to obtain equivalent service potential.

The lecture also discusses capital concepts. Under financial capital, capital is viewed as the entity’s net assets or equity, and profit exists only after the entity has maintained the dollar value or purchasing power of opening equity. Under physical capital, capital is viewed as the operating capability of the entity’s assets, and profit exists only after the entity has maintained enough capacity to continue operating at the same level. These concepts affect how profit is understood and measured.

A major theoretical point in the lecture is that accounting uses a modified historical cost system, not a pure historical cost system. Many items begin at historical cost, but accounting standards may later require or permit adjustments using other measurement bases. This reflects the tension between reliability and relevance. Historical cost is often more verifiable, while fair value and other current measures may be more relevant. Financial reporting therefore balances competing qualities rather than relying on one perfect measurement syste

**8. CASH MANAGEMENT CONTROL; CASH FLOW STATEMENTS**

Theory: 
Cash is treated as a highly important asset because almost every business transaction eventually leads to either an inflow or an outflow of cash. In a broad accounting sense, cash includes money, EFTPOS and credit card transaction evidence, cheques, postal notes, and other negotiable instruments that a bank will normally accept as deposits. For the statement of cash flows, the definition is more formal: cash includes cash on hand and demand deposits, while cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and subject to insignificant risk of value change. Entities must disclose the policy they use to decide what counts as cash and cash equivalents.

The lecture treats cash as both necessary and risky. Cash is necessary because the business needs it to operate, settle obligations, respond to opportunities, and maintain liquidity. At the same time, cash is risky because it is the asset most vulnerable to theft and misappropriation. It is also an unproductive asset when held idle because cash sitting unused generally produces little or no income. Therefore, cash management is about balance: the entity must hold enough cash to remain liquid and safe, but not so much idle cash that resources are wasted.

The lecture also links cash flow to ESG. Strong environmental, social and governance practices can improve cash flows through top-line growth, cost reductions, reduced regulatory and legal intervention, improved employee productivity, and better investment and asset optimisation. The theory is that ESG is not only a reputational issue; it can affect the economic quality and risk profile of a business, which can then affect cash flows, financing costs, investor confidence, and long-term sustainability.

Internal control is central to cash management. The main purpose of internal control over cash is to reduce the risk of error, theft, fraud, and unauthorised transactions. Two core principles dominate the theory: first, the person responsible for handling or having custody of cash should not also be responsible for maintaining the records about cash; second, cash records should be verified frequently using independent evidence, especially the bank’s records. This means cash control is not just about trusting employees; it is about designing a system where duties are separated, evidence exists, and independent checks make concealment difficult.

The control of cash receipts is based on the same principle of separating custody from record keeping. Cash receipts must be supported by evidence, recorded promptly, and checked against actual money or bank deposits. The theory is that cash received by a business passes through a vulnerable point: if the same person receives cash, records it, deposits it, and reconciles the records, errors or theft can be hidden. Therefore, good control assigns different responsibilities to different people and uses documents, registers, receipts, EFTPOS records, deposit slips, and bank records as independent support.

Cash short and over exists because real cash-handling systems can produce differences between expected cash and actual cash. The theory is not that every difference is fraud, but that differences must be identified, recorded, and investigated when necessary. A shortage indicates actual cash is less than expected; an overage indicates actual cash is more than expected. Over a period, net shortages are treated as a cost of operating or a sundry expense, while net overs are treated as other income. However, repeated or large differences indicate weak internal control, poor staff training, error, or possible manipulation.

Cash payments require strong control because unauthorised payments can remove cash from the business very quickly. The theory is that approving payment, making payment, and recording payment should be separated. The employee who approves invoices should not also prepare cheques or electronic transfers. The employee who signs cheques or authorises electronic transfers should not also approve invoices or maintain accounting records. This prevents one person from creating, approving, paying, and hiding a false or improper transaction.

Bank accounts are important because they create an independent external record of cash activity. The bank’s record provides a check against the entity’s internal cash records. This is why maintaining a bank account is itself a control feature. The bank records deposits received, cheques paid, and electronic transfers, which allows the business to compare its records against an external source. The theory is that a bank reconciliation is not simply a calculation; it is an internal control process that explains differences between the business’s cash records and the bank’s records.

A bank reconciliation exists because the business’s cash records and the bank statement often do not agree at the same date. The disagreement is usually caused by timing differences, bank-originated items, and errors. Timing differences occur when the business has recorded something that the bank has not yet processed, or when the bank has processed something that the business has not yet recorded. Bank-originated items include charges, interest, direct transfers, dishonoured cheques, and other items first seen on the bank statement. Errors may be made by either the bank or the business. The theoretical purpose of the reconciliation is to prove that the difference is explainable, not to assume one side is automatically correct.

The bank statement appears opposite to the business’s ledger because it is written from the bank’s point of view. For the business, cash at bank is an asset. For the bank, the customer’s deposit is a liability because the bank owes that money to the customer. This is why a favourable bank statement balance appears as a credit from the bank’s perspective, while a favourable cash at bank balance appears as a debit from the business’s perspective. The theory is that reconciliation requires understanding both viewpoints: the entity’s internal records and the bank’s external records describe the same relationship from opposite sides.

Petty cash is a control system for small, incidental payments. The theory is that it would be inefficient to use cheques or electronic transfers for every minor expense, so a small controlled cash fund is established. A specific employee, the petty cashier, is responsible for that fund. The system relies on limited access, documentary evidence, authorisation, vouchers, and periodic reimbursement. Petty cash is not meant to be an uncontrolled pool of money; it is a small, fixed, accountable fund that allows convenience while still maintaining control.

A cash budget is a planning and control tool. It forecasts future cash receipts and future cash payments over a period. The purpose is to help management predict whether the entity will have enough cash to meet obligations as they fall due. The theory is forward-looking: rather than waiting for a cash shortage to happen, management estimates future inflows and outflows so it can plan borrowing, delay non-urgent payments, accelerate collections, invest surplus cash, or adjust spending. A cash budget deals only with cash movements, so non-cash expenses and non-cash revenues are not part of the cash budget logic.

Cash budgeting supports good management because it improves liquidity planning, credit reputation, borrowing decisions, expenditure control, and investment of idle funds. If a cash budget shows a future shortage, management can arrange finance before the shortage becomes urgent. If it shows a future surplus, management can invest the excess or use it productively. Therefore, the cash budget is both a planning tool and an internal control tool. It encourages discipline over when cash is received, when cash is paid, and how much cash should be retained.

The lecture identifies essential principles of cash management. The business should reduce the collection time for accounts receivable, postpone payments to accounts payable without damaging supplier relationships, keep inventory levels as low as practical without harming operations, invest surplus cash, and plan capital expenditure. The theory is that cash is improved not only by increasing sales, but by improving the timing and efficiency of working capital. Faster customer collection, sensible supplier payment timing, efficient inventory management, and planned investment decisions all improve the cash position of the business.

Cash adequacy is the idea that cash flows from operations should be assessed to determine whether the business can meet its obligations. The lecture identifies short-term cash flow adequacy and general cash flow adequacy as measures used to assess solvency. The theoretical focus is not simply whether the business made profit, but whether its operations generate enough cash to satisfy current and non-current obligations. A business with positive accounting profit but weak operating cash flow may still be financially vulnerable.

The statement of cash flows is important because it provides information that the income statement and balance sheet alone cannot provide. It identifies cash inflows and outflows from operating, investing, and financing activities. This matters because profit can look very different from operating cash flow, especially where credit sales are delayed or not collected. The statement of cash flows therefore helps users understand the relationship between profitability and actual cash generation.

The purpose of the statement of cash flows is broader than simply showing the change in cash. Used with the other financial statements, it helps users evaluate changes in net assets, assess liquidity and solvency, understand the entity’s financial structure, assess the ability to generate future cash flows, check the accuracy of previous cash flow predictions, compare performance across entities, and assess the entity’s flexibility in responding to changing circumstances.

A key theory from the lecture is that cash flow information is often less affected by accounting method choices than profit. Profit can be influenced by accruals, estimates, depreciation methods, inventory methods, provisions, and recognition choices. Cash flow is not free from classification issues, but actual cash received and paid is generally more concrete. This is why the lecturer says cash flow helps users compare entities and assess whether the entity can adapt to changing circumstances.

The statement of cash flows is structured around three classifications: operating activities, investing activities, and financing activities. Operating activities are the principal revenue-producing activities of the entity and other activities that are not investing or financing. Investing activities relate to acquiring and disposing of long-term assets and investments that are not cash equivalents. Financing activities relate to changes in the size and composition of the entity’s equity and borrowings. The theory is that users need to know not only whether cash increased or decreased, but also why it changed.

Operating cash flows are considered especially important because they show whether the entity’s main business activities generate cash. A business cannot rely indefinitely on selling assets, borrowing money, or receiving owner contributions. In theory, a healthy continuing business should eventually generate cash from its core operations. Operating cash flow therefore provides a strong signal about the sustainability of the business model.

Investing cash flows explain how the business is using cash to acquire or dispose of long-term resources. These flows help users assess whether the business is expanding, replacing assets, selling assets, or changing its investment structure. Investing outflows are not automatically negative in economic meaning, because they may represent future capacity. Investing inflows are not automatically positive, because they may indicate asset sales caused by pressure. The theoretical meaning depends on context.

Financing cash flows explain how the entity is funded by owners and lenders. They show whether the entity is raising capital, borrowing, repaying debt, returning funds to owners, or changing its financial structure. This matters because operating cash flow alone does not show how the business is financed. Financing flows help users assess dependence on external finance, debt repayment pressure, and distributions to owners.

The general format of the statement of cash flows requires separate disclosure of cash inflows and outflows classified as operating, investing, or financing. The net cash flow for each classification is then totalled to determine the net increase or decrease in cash for the period. Certain cash flows, such as interest received and paid, dividends received and paid, and income tax paid, must be separately disclosed and classified consistently from period to period.

The lecture distinguishes the direct method and the indirect method. Both are allowed under IASB/AASB rules, but the direct method is encouraged. The direct method presents operating cash flows by showing major classes of cash receipts and cash payments. The indirect method starts with profit and adjusts it to arrive at net cash from operating activities. The theoretical difference is presentation: direct method focuses on actual operating cash categories, while indirect method explains the difference between profit and operating cash flow.

The notes to the statement of cash flows are theoretically important because the main statement cannot explain everything. Notes disclose the policy for cash and cash equivalents, reconcile operating cash flow to profit or loss where required, and disclose relevant non-cash financing and investing activities. Non-cash activities matter because they may significantly change the entity’s resources and obligations even though no cash moved at the time. The statement’s purpose is to provide relevant information about operating, investing and financing activities, and notes support that purpose.

The lecture also covers advanced classification issues. Some cash flows can be classified differently depending on the nature of the entity. For one type of business, interest or dividends received may be incidental investing cash flows; for another, especially a financial institution, they may be part of normal operating activity. The theory is that classification depends on the economic substance of the cash flow in relation to the entity’s main operations, not only on the name of the transaction.

GST, trade receivables, trade payables, discounts, non-trade receivables and payables, short-term investments, dividends, and non-current asset sales all create classification and interpretation issues in cash flow reporting. The theory behind these issues is that the statement of cash flows reports cash movements according to their economic nature. Some items relate to operating activity, some relate to investing activity, and some relate to financing activity. The accountant must classify based on what caused the cash movement and what part of the business it relates to.

The statement of cash flows has limitations. It reports past cash flows, not guaranteed future cash flows. It does not fully capture non-cash transactions in the body of the statement. It may not by itself prove long-term liquidity or solvency. It also depends on classification choices, and users must read it with the other financial statements and notes. Therefore, the statement of cash flows is highly useful, but it is not a complete replacement for the income statement, statement of financial position, or broader business analysis.

Accounting Format:
Bank Reconciliation Statement

For a bank reconciliation statement, use a three-line heading. Write the business name on the first line, Bank Reconciliation Statement on the second line, and as at [date] on the third line. Use “as at” because a bank reconciliation is prepared for one specific date.

Start with Balance as per bank statement, not the Cash at Bank ledger. Clearly label the bank statement balance as Dr or Cr. A bank statement Cr means the bank shows cash available. A bank statement Dr means the bank shows an overdraft. The bank statement is from the bank’s perspective, so it is opposite to the business’s Cash at Bank ledger.

Use the exemplar method. If the bank statement starts with a Cr balance, add deposits not credited and deduct unpresented cheques or outstanding EFTs. If the bank statement starts with a Dr overdraft, add unpresented cheques or outstanding EFTs because they make the overdraft worse, and deduct deposits not credited because they reduce the overdraft.

List each unpresented cheque or outstanding EFT separately if details are given. Show the cheque number or EFT label, then the amount. After listing them, total the group. Bank errors are included in the bank reconciliation because they are errors made by the bank. Adjust the bank error in the direction needed to correct the bank statement balance.

Do not include business-record items directly in the bank reconciliation unless the question specifically requires it. Items such as bank fees, interest, direct deposits, dishonoured cheques, direct debits, and business errors usually require updating the Cash Receipts Journal, Cash Payments Journal, or Cash at Bank ledger first. The bank reconciliation mainly adjusts for timing differences and bank errors.

Finish with Balance as per Cash at Bank account or Cash at Bank balance. Clearly label the final balance as Dr or Cr. A final Cash at Bank Dr means the business has cash at bank. A final Cash at Bank Cr means the business has a bank overdraft.

Use a neat vertical layout with Add: and Less: sections. Indent sub-items such as cheque numbers under the relevant heading. Show subtotals after grouped items and underline the final answer.

Statement of Cash Flows — Direct Method

For a statement of cash flows, use a three-line heading. Write the business name on the first line, Statement of Cash Flows on the second line, and for the year ended [date] on the third line. Use “for the year ended” because the statement covers a period of time.

The statement must be split into three sections in this order:

Cash flows from operating activities
Cash flows from investing activities
Cash flows from financing activities

Under the direct method, the operating section shows actual cash receipts and actual cash payments from normal business operations. Start with cash receipts from customers. Then list cash payments such as cash paid to suppliers, employees, wages, rent, tax, or other operating payments. Cash inflows are shown as positive amounts. Cash outflows are shown in brackets.

End the operating section with Net cash from operating activities if positive, or Net cash used in operating activities if negative.

The investing section shows cash flows from long-term assets and investments. Purchases of equipment, vehicles, land, buildings, and investments are outflows and should be shown in brackets. Proceeds from selling equipment, investments, or non-current assets are inflows and should be shown as positive amounts. Interest received may be classified as investing in your course examples unless the question says otherwise.

End the investing section with Net cash from investing activities or Net cash used in investing activities.

The financing section shows cash flows from owners and lenders. Owner contributions, share issues, loans received, and mortgages received are inflows. Drawings, dividends paid, loan repayments, and mortgage repayments are outflows and should be shown in brackets.

End the financing section with Net cash from financing activities or Net cash used in financing activities.

After the three sections, calculate the overall cash movement. Show Net increase/decrease in cash and cash equivalents, then add Cash and cash equivalents at beginning of period, then finish with Cash and cash equivalents at end of period. The final closing cash figure must agree with the cash figure in the notes or statement of financial position.

Statement of Cash Flows — Indirect Method

The indirect method uses the same overall statement structure as the direct method. The investing section, financing section, net increase/decrease in cash, opening cash, and closing cash are prepared the same way.

The only major difference is the operating activities section. Instead of listing cash receipts and cash payments, the indirect method starts with Profit for the period and adjusts it to convert accrual profit into net cash from operating activities.

Start with Profit for the period. Then add back non-cash expenses such as depreciation, amortisation, impairment, and provisions. These are added back because they reduced profit but did not involve cash outflow.

Then adjust for gains and losses on sale of non-current assets. Subtract gains because they increased profit but are not operating cash flows. Add losses because they reduced profit but are not operating cash flows. The actual cash proceeds from selling the asset belong in the investing section.

Then adjust for changes in current assets and current liabilities. Use this rule:

Increase in current asset = subtract
Decrease in current asset = add
Increase in current liability = add
Decrease in current liability = subtract

Examples of current assets include accounts receivable, inventory, and prepayments. Examples of current liabilities include accounts payable, wages payable, expenses payable, and interest payable.

End the operating section with Net cash from operating activities. This figure should be the same operating cash flow figure produced under the direct method.
